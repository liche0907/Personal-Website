Teaching statement

What students should leave with

I treat my own teaching as something to measure. I run my own anonymous surveys each course, record my sections, and work on one habit at a time; right now that is pacing and spending less time on the slides and more with the students. For most of my students, introductory economics is the only economics course they will ever take. My aim is that they leave able to use a handful of ideas (trade-offs, incentives, what a price is telling you) on decisions they actually face. My teaching rests on four principles.

Principle 1

Interaction

Students learn by doing. Introductory classes should be driven by games and hands-on practice throughout, using in-class activities and quizzes that run on students' own devices. In technical or advanced classes, producing code and output becomes mandatory: students apply a method from the course to a real-world question of their choice, such as an essay analyzing Covid-19 in a macroeconomics class, or a short study of demographics, labor or health using Current Population Survey data, with tools they learned.

In design

An in-class digital game that students play on their own devices.

Principle 2

A reality check

Concepts should connect to something students recognize from their own lives. And they need to walk away with real applications of the knowledge in the real world. For example, they can learn how to quantify the supply shock of Iran War, even at an introductory level.

The slide on the right uses one McDonald's market to show why every restaurant ends up the same size in the long run. Step through it the way students saw it in class.

Principle 3

Attention

Understanding a topic in class is far more efficient than learning from slides after class. Occasional clips from TV help catch students' attention and test how well they can apply concepts. I tend to build a full case around it utilizing important concepts or logics.

On the right, from a discussion on welfare analysis: students watch a negotiation scene, then work out who is buying, who gets the larger surplus, and who is more elastic (has stronger bargaining power).

Principle 4

AI as part of learning

AI should be an important part of learning, built into course materials rather than used to complete assignments. Students can use it to get past technical obstacles, such as debugging code or drawing diagrams, to ask questions about concepts, to generate practice questions, and to evaluate their own thinking and original work. How much AI to allow depends on the level. In introductory courses, it should be kept to a minimum and allowed only for debugging, because good habits and a sense of technical responsibility need to be instilled early. In advanced courses, AI use is expected and even encouraged, but accountability stays with the student: they build and maintain their own code base independently, and understand it well enough to revisit it at any point in their careers. At every level, exams and quizzes are strictly AI-free.

In design

I plan to learn how to design the full course AI policy through UCR's University Teaching Certificate program, Winter–Spring 2027.
